What the numbers say

Finding 01 — one genuine risk-adjusted win

Long-term reversal returned 22.54% a year against buy-and-hold's 23.13% — slightly less — but did it with a 22.9% drawdown instead of 37.8%. It beats holding on Sharpe (1.45 vs 1.01), Sortino (2.20 vs 1.43), Calmar (0.98 vs 0.61) and Ulcer (7.06 vs 11.81). Nearly the same return for substantially less pain.

Finding 02 — diversification went the wrong way

Running all twelve together returned 13.11% with a 42.8% drawdown — worse than four of its own components, and the deepest drawdown in the study. Blending the strong strategies with the weak ones diluted rather than diversified.

Finding 03 — FX and futures added nothing

Holding all 528 instruments returned 23.13%. Holding the 500 equities alone returned 23.75%. The 28 currency and futures instruments diluted the book rather than diversifying it.

Hold it, or trade it?

If you had just held everything

23.13% a year, turning into a 700% total return — but with a 37.8% peak-to-trough fall along the way, and 23.2% annual volatility. Simple, cheap, and hard to sit through.

If you had traded the best rule

22.54% a year from long-term reversal, with a 22.9% worst fall and 14.8% volatility. You give up almost nothing in return and roughly 40% of the drawdown.

The calmest books

Low volatility and low beta drew down just 11.3% and 17.0% — less than half of holding — but returned only ~4% a year. They work exactly as designed; the design just isn't competitive here.

The most active

Band reversion traded 1,323 times for 5.89% a year and a 39% drawdown. Turn-of-month traded 794 times for 11.69%. Turnover did not buy quality.

Read this before quoting any number

These caveats are not boilerplate. Each one would change how a figure above should be read, and the first two would change it a lot.

1

Everything here is in-sample

These rules were chosen knowing how this decade went. An out-of-sample split was run — the last column — but a reporting bug meant the underlying figures for each half failed to record. The verdicts are real; the detail behind them is missing and is being re-run.

2

Survivorship bias

Only instruments that still exist today can be tested. Companies that delisted or went bust are invisible. This flatters every row in the table, including buy-and-hold.

3

The futures are mis-sized

All 20 futures are sized as though their full notional were paid in cash — no margin, no contract multiplier. Both return and risk are understated against a real futures account. Treat them as price-series research, not as a futures book.

4

One market, one decade

US-listed instruments over roughly 2016–2026, a period dominated by one of the strongest equity bull runs on record. Long-term reversal buys multi-year laggards, which is exactly the trade that decade rewarded.

5

Modelled fills, no intrabar data

When a single bar's range covers both the stop and the target, the stop is always assumed — deliberately pessimistic. Slippage and commission are estimates.

What isn't here, and why

Nine strategies from the original library were not built. In every case the data required would have produced numbers that look real and aren't.

  • Value, quality, profitability, QMJ, low-investment — the available fundamentals are point-in-time only, so backtesting them would inject look-ahead bias.
  • Post-earnings drift, earnings premium — the data feed returns four quarters of earnings surprise, not the decade a test would need.
  • Pairs / statistical arbitrage — needs a two-instrument execution model the engine does not have.
  • Carry — needs futures term structure; only front-month series are available.
  • Options strategies — no usable historical options data.

Research and decision-support only. Nothing here was executed, and nothing here is financial advice. Past behaviour is not predictive; a strategy that worked historically can stop working the moment conditions change. Data may be delayed or inaccurate.
